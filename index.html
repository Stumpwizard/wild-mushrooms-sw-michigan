Spore prints · methods & identification limits

A spore print is a visible deposit of microscopic spores. Its color helps narrow an identification, but no spore color proves that a mushroom is edible or safe.

Make a basic print

Supplies: a fresh, mature mushroom, clean foil, paper or glass, a cup or bowl, and a label.

  1. Document first. Photograph the intact mushroom, underside and stem base. Keep the stem and base for identification.
  2. Prepare the cap. Remove the stem so the cap sits flat. For a large specimen, use a cap section; keep each specimen separate.
  3. Face the underside down. Rest the gills or pores on the printing surface. Fresh boletes can be printed this way too.
  4. Cover and wait. Shelter it under an inverted cup or bowl to limit drafts. Leave undisturbed for 2–24 hours, often overnight. A drop of water on a dry cap can help; avoid soaking it.
  5. Lift and record. Lift the cap straight up. Photograph the deposit and label it with the date, collection location and matching specimen number.

Keep the stem attached: pass it through a hole in an index card supported over a cup, with the cap underside resting on the card.

Choose a surface

  • White paper: useful for dark deposits. A dark sheet alongside it makes pale spores easier to notice.
  • Clear glass or a microscope slide: move white and dark backgrounds underneath to compare the same deposit. Use glass with safe edges.
  • Aluminum foil: convenient for collecting and saving a deposit.

Read the result carefully

Judge the deposited spores, not the cap or gill color. Compare with a reliable identification key; subtle shades can be difficult to interpret. Habitat, stem-base features and other characteristics still matter, and microscopy may be necessary.

No visible print? The specimen may be immature, dried out or past active spore release. Try another fresh, mature cap. An absent or very faint deposit is not evidence of white spores.

Other forms: hard brackets may not print readily. Morels can leave spores around a cap section as well as beneath it. Puffballs do not use this cap-down method; never squeeze them to make a spore cloud.

Handling: do not inhale or blow on spores. Keep study specimens away from food, children and pets, and wash hands afterward. Never taste an unknown mushroom to identify it.

Sustainable harvesting & ethical foraging

Protect the habitat, respect access rules, and bring home only what you can identify and use.

Before you collect

  • Get permission. Ask the landowner before entering or collecting on private property. For parks, forests and preserves, check the specific land manager’s current rules, permits, limits and closures; some places prohibit collecting.
  • Know Michigan’s distinction. Foods harvested from state lands are for personal use, not resale. Selling mushrooms collected elsewhere involves separate Michigan training and certification requirements.
  • Learn the whole mushroom. Photograph its habitat, underside and stem base, and record its substrate and nearby trees. Join a local expert-led foray. A photo match or an edibility badge cannot establish that your specimen is safe.

Sources: Michigan DNR: access and rules ↗ · NAMA: responsible foraging ↗

Leave a healthy patch

  • Take only what you will use. Leave some mushrooms for wildlife, reproduction and other visitors. Leave immature, decaying and unneeded specimens in place. Photograph unusual finds rather than collecting them casually.
  • Protect the forest floor. Watch your footing around a patch. Do not rake or scrape leaf litter, strip bark, or break apart logs to reach mushrooms. Avoid disturbing the underground fungal network and its habitat.
  • Collect carefully. For a permitted identification specimen, preserve the entire stem base; it may hold decisive features. Keep study specimens separate from food. Once identification is established, trim dirt from food specimens before packing them.
  • Respect people and places. Pack out litter, leave gates as you found them, and avoid blocking trails. Ask before sharing locations on private land.

Sources: NAMA: collecting and habitat care ↗ · UAF Extension: harvest practices ↗

Handling, storage & cooking wild mushrooms

Identify first, prepare second. Have every specimen confirmed in person by a qualified expert before treating it as food. Cooking, drying or freezing cannot reliably make a poisonous mushroom safe. A “Medicinal use” badge is not a recommendation to eat that species.

From the woods to the refrigerator

  1. Keep the harvest in good condition. Use a clean basket or breathable food container, avoid crushing specimens, and get them into refrigeration promptly. Keep different species and unidentified study material separate.
  2. Check freshness. Discard moldy, slimy, decaying or badly damaged mushrooms. Do not try to rescue spoiled specimens by cooking them.
  3. Store cold and breathable. Refrigerate fresh mushrooms in a paper bag at 40°F (4°C) or below. Avoid a sealed plastic bag. Use promptly; UAF Extension gives fresh morels about 2–3 refrigerated days.
  4. Clean just before preparation. Wash your hands and utensils. Trim soil from confirmed food specimens, brush away debris, and rinse as needed under clean running water; pat dry. Split morels lengthwise to inspect the interior and remove insects or grit. Avoid washing morels before storage.

Sources: FDA: freshness and refrigeration ↗ · Michigan DNR: cleaning and preparation ↗ · UAF Extension: morel storage ↗

Cool and preserve properly

  • Chill leftovers promptly. Put them in shallow containers and refrigerate or freeze within 2 hours of cooking, or 1 hour when the temperature is above 90°F (32°C). Thaw frozen portions in the refrigerator.
  • Freeze or dry with a suitable method. Follow Extension instructions for the identified species. For morels, cook before freezing; cool promptly and label portions with species and date. For drying, use fresh, sound morels and dry until brittle, then keep in airtight food containers. Dried morels still require thorough cooking.
  • Do not home-can wild mushrooms. The National Center for Home Food Preservation explicitly excludes wild mushrooms from its mushroom-canning directions. Do not substitute them into a cultivated-mushroom canning recipe.

Sources: FDA: cooling and thawing ↗ · UAF Extension: freezing and drying morels ↗ · NCHFP: canning limitation ↗

Cook for the identified species

  • Cook edible wild mushrooms thoroughly. Do not serve them raw or merely warmed. Use a reliable recipe for the exact species; preparation requirements differ, and one cooking time does not apply to every mushroom in this guide.
  • Prepare even pieces. Slice or divide confirmed edible mushrooms so pieces cook evenly. For a sauté, use butter or oil and manageable batches; follow the species-specific recipe through full cooking before adding mushrooms to a dish that will only be warmed briefly.
  • Try a modest portion of a newly learned edible species. Even correctly identified, cooked mushrooms can cause individual reactions. Keep the first serving simple, with one mushroom species. A small taste is never an identification or poison test.

Example: confirmed true morels

After cleaning and halving, sauté slowly in butter or oil until thoroughly cooked and tender. UAF Extension’s example for a half-pound batch takes about 10–15 minutes. Treat that as a recipe example, not a universal safety timer; size, batch and heat matter.

Morel caution: never serve raw or undercooked morels. FDA states that proper cooking can reduce illness risk but cannot guarantee safety. False morels are not made safe by following a true-morel recipe; do not eat them.

Sources: Michigan DNR: cooking and individual reactions ↗ · UAF Extension: sautéed morels ↗ · FDA: morel safety limits ↗

Illness after eating mushrooms? Contact a healthcare professional or call U.S. Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 promptly.

What the edibility & medicinal labels mean

✓ Edible speciesEaten when correctly identified and appropriately prepared. This does not mean a photographed specimen is safe to eat.

✕ ToxicPoisonous; do not eat.

✕ Nonedible as a table mushroomUnsuitable for ordinary table use, often because of tough or insubstantial flesh. This label does not mean poisonous.

Food use not recommended hereEdibility is uncertain, disputed, or requires specialist advice. These entries are for observation; this guide does not endorse eating them.

Medicinal use Evidence variesMarks documented traditional use or human investigation, including studies with negative results. It does not mean proven benefit, FDA approval, edibility, or a safe identification. “Traditional use” describes history; “Human studies” describes research, not a positive result. Laboratory-only activity by itself is not enough for this badge.

Medicinal use: Evidence varies. Each marked entry explains the reported use, evidence and cautions. Studied extracts are not equivalent to wild mushrooms or homemade preparations. Discuss supplements with a clinician; do not replace prescribed treatment. Search “medicinal” to find the marked entries.

Scope: This is a curated regional guide, not a census of every species in southwest Michigan. Many groups require microscopy or DNA for species level identification. The seasonal windows reflect typical southwest Michigan fruiting, not a current forecast. Months are approximate and shift with rain and temperature. Common names vary by region and may refer to more than one species. Photos show examples, never a complete identification.

Safety: Toxic and deadly species are included for recognition. Never use an app, one photo, or an edibility label to decide what to eat. Michigan DNR recommends learning with an experienced guide. DNR identification guidance · Foraging rules · Berrien County observations.

Before you use this guide

A wrong mushroom identification can be fatal.

  • For learning and comparison only. Photos, descriptions, feature matches and green “Edible species” checks cannot confirm that your mushroom is safe to eat.
  • Deadly lookalikes can appear nearly identical. Mistakes can cause severe poisoning, organ failure or death. This guide is incomplete; an absent warning does not mean a mushroom is safe.
  • Cooking does not make poisonous mushrooms safe. Symptoms of poisoning can be delayed for hours.
  • Never decide to eat a mushroom using this guide alone. Have every specimen identified in person by a qualified mushroom expert before considering consumption.

Suspected mushroom poisoning? In the U.S., call Poison Control immediately at 1-800-222-1222. Do not wait for symptoms.

Call 911 if someone collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be awakened.
